Battery wiring: LiPo → TP4056 → diode → ESP32-C3

The car runs on its 200 mAh LiPo, and the C3's own USB-C stays usable for flashing and serial with the battery connected. The Schottky diode D1 is what makes that safe: current can go from the battery to the board, never from USB back into the cell.

What happens when you plug in

Whichever side has the higher voltage powers the ESP32. The C3's USB puts about 5 V on the 5V pin, always above the battery (4.2 V at most), so D1 blocks and the battery is left alone.

Bring-up with a multimeter

  1. ESP32 not connected yet. Charge the cell: CHG LED red → DONE when full. B+ to B− ≈ 4.2 V.
  2. OUT+ to OUT− ≈ cell voltage. 0 V means the protection tripped — plug the charger once to reset it.
  3. SW1 on: D1 cathode to OUT− ≈ cell − 0.3 V.
  4. Connect 5V and GND to the C3. It boots on battery (OLED splash).
  5. Plug the C3's USB with SW1 still on. Across D1 the cathode must read higher than the anode — proof that USB isn't reaching the cell.
  6. Flash over USB with the battery on. It must work exactly like on the bench.

Voltage budget: the 5V pin sees the cell minus ~0.3 V, so below a cell of about 3.6 V the 3V3 rail sags with it. The C3 keeps running to about 3.0 V and most of a LiPo's charge sits above 3.5 V, so little is lost; a P-MOSFET power-path would remove the drop if it ever matters. Rough runtime on 200 mAh: about 4–5 h. It was half that until the WiFi hotspot was removed — an AP the C3 can never sleep off costs 80–100 mA, more than everything else on the car put together. Estimated, not yet measured on the cell.

Board facts from espboards.dev. It does not say whether the C3's 5V pin is tied straight to USB VBUS or goes through a diode; this wiring is safe either way, because D1 blocks anything on the 5V rail from reaching the cell.
